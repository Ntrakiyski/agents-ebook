# Creator Profiles

This folder contains creator transcripts, essays, and course-style material for
**Beyond Chat**.

These files have two different roles:

- **Teachers:** Dan Koe and Matt Pocock shape teaching style, pacing, structure,
  reader energy, and chapter flow.
- **Knowledge references:** Nate Herk, AI Engineer, and Stanford CS329A shape
  content direction, principles, technical examples, and research questions.

These files are reference material, not manuscript source. Use them to learn
patterns, audience language, useful teaching moves, and technical directions.
Do not copy prose, transcript wording, titles, or diagrams into the book.

## Folder Layout

- `dan-koe/` - creator essays and teaching-style references.
- `matt-pocock/` - AI-assisted software workflow transcripts.
- `nate-herk/` - AI automation and second-brain creator transcripts.
- `ai-engineer/` - AI Engineer talks and production-facing transcripts.
- `stanford-cs329a/` - Stanford CS329A self-improving AI agents transcripts.

## Teachers

### Dan Koe

Useful for reader energy and page rhythm.

Observed patterns:

- Promise-first openings that begin with a felt limitation.
- Short sections with clear conceptual steps.
- Named protocols and simple frames that make abstract work feel actionable.
- Identity and agency framing: the reader is not just consuming information;
  they are becoming someone who can build and direct systems.
- Simple-to-technical mapping, where the concept starts human and becomes
  operational.

Use this influence when shaping chapter openings, checkpoints, and transitions
from "I use ChatGPT" to "I can design an AI system."

### Matt Pocock

Useful for disciplined build workflow.

Observed patterns:

- Work moves through explicit phases: idea, research, prototype, specification,
  tickets, implementation, and review.
- Planning artifacts are treated as executable scaffolding, not paperwork.
- Prototypes are used to discover taste and expose vague requirements early.
- The serious use of AI is framed as engineering leverage, not passive
  delegation.
- Review and QA generate the next round of work instead of ending the process.

Use this influence for ATLAS build loops, chapter deliverables, project
checkpoints, and the recurring "break it, inspect it, improve it" structure.

## Knowledge References

### Nate Herk

Useful for content direction around the AI-curious reader, AI automation, and
second-brain positioning.

Observed patterns:

- Speaks directly to people who have watched many AI videos but have not yet
  turned that knowledge into working systems.
- Frames AI learning around practical automation, second brains, delivery, and
  business usefulness.
- Uses accessible hooks and concrete outcomes before introducing deeper
  technical detail.
- Treats tools as a way to make people more capable, not as trivia to memorize.

Use this reference to keep the content grounded in the primary reader: someone
who knows the vocabulary from videos but needs a guided path into real building.

### AI Engineer

Useful for current industry examples and research leads.

Observed themes:

- Memory systems, context engineering, evals, computer use, agent security,
  long-running agents, and AI-native company operations.
- Production claims often point toward concrete systems, papers, repositories,
  or engineering lessons that should be verified from primary sources before
  becoming manuscript claims.
- The material is especially useful for understanding where contemporary tools
  implement durable primitives.

Use this influence as a discovery layer for evidence. Bind important claims to
official docs, papers, source code, or first-hand engineering sources before a
chapter becomes draft-ready.

### Stanford CS329A

Useful for rigor around self-improving AI agents.

Observed themes:

- Self-improvement loops, test-time compute, verification, feedback, tool use,
  code generation, planning, and evaluation.
- The material helps connect ATLAS to durable research ideas without turning
  the book into an academic course.
- Best used to support the later layers of the book: harnesses, evals,
  autonomy, multi-agent systems, and learning systems.

Use this influence when converting practical ATLAS chapters into technically
defensible mental models.

## Book-Level Style Synthesis

The book should teach with:

- Dan Koe's momentum and identity-level framing.
- Matt Pocock's disciplined AI build workflow.

The book's content and principles should be informed by:

- Nate Herk's bridge from AI video consumer to practical builder.
- AI Engineer's production reality and current system examples.
- Stanford CS329A's research-backed rigor.

The result should still sound like **Beyond Chat**, not like any one source.
Every chapter should keep the reader moving through a real ATLAS upgrade:

1. Name the limitation.
2. Build the next capability.
3. Break the naive version.
4. Inspect what happened.
5. Evaluate improvement.
6. Lock the upgrade into ATLAS.
7. Show what comes next.
